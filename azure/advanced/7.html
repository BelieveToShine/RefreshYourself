Naming services first is designing backwards

Given a set of requirements, what architecture questions would you actually ask first?

Why it matters ▾
  • A vague "design a system for X" prompt is deliberately open-ended — real architecture conversations start with clarifying questions, not a service name.
  • Jumping straight to service names is one of the clearest signals interviewers use to tell junior enthusiasm apart from senior/architect judgment.
  • This mirrors how real design work actually happens on the job — requirements come first, technology choices come after, every single time.
🔥 Easy interview recall

Availability → drives HA / DR choices

Load shape → drives the scaling strategy

Data sensitivity → drives identity & region choices

Latency → drives placement & caching

Budget → drives the tradeoffs across all four

Think: the service name is the last thing decided, not the first.

🧠 Visual Mental Model — understand it in 5 seconds
⚠️ Common Trap

"We'll use Cosmos DB and Front Door" is not a strong opening answer. A senior/architect interviewer is listening for whether you ask clarifying questions first — not for how many service names you can list.

🔄 Likely Follow-up

"What's a good first question if the prompt is genuinely vague?" Something like "What does downtime actually cost this business, and how much of it is tolerable?" — a question that reveals which tradeoffs actually matter before any design commitment is made.

🎯 Say this in the interview — the exact lines to land

"Ask about availability, load shape, data sensitivity, latency, and budget before naming a single service — that ordering is exactly what a senior interviewer is listening for."

  • Availability → how much downtime is tolerable drives the HA/DR choice.
  • Load shape → steady vs. spiky drives the scaling strategy.
  • Data sensitivity → compliance & residency drives identity, encryption, and region.
  • Latency → regional placement & caching drives CDN use.
  • Budget → the tradeoffs across all four above, not a line item on its own.
  • Naming a service before any of this is designing backwards — the exact thing being probed for.
📖 Explanation ▾

Before naming any specific Azure service, a real architecture conversation opens with a small set of questions that shape every decision that follows: availability needs (what downtime is actually acceptable — this drives the HA/DR choice), expected load and its shape (steady traffic vs. spiky, like a flash sale — this drives the scaling strategy), data sensitivity and compliance (this drives identity, encryption, and region/residency choices), latency requirements (this drives regional placement, caching, and CDN use), and budget constraints (this drives the tradeoffs across all of the above). Naming a specific service before establishing these is designing backwards.

This is exactly what a senior or architect interviewer is checking for in an open-ended "design X" prompt — not how many service names a candidate can produce, but whether they ask the questions that determine which services would even make sense. A candidate who asks "what does downtime cost this business, and how much of it is tolerable" before proposing anything is showing the same judgment they'd need to apply on a real project, where getting these answers wrong is far more expensive than getting a service name wrong.

🌍 Real-world example ▾

Asked to "design a scalable e-commerce backend," a strong candidate doesn't open with a service name. They ask about expected peak traffic first — is there a flash sale or a seasonal spike that changes the load shape — and about data residency requirements, since those two answers alone already rule certain designs in or out. Only after that does a specific architecture get proposed, and it comes with a reason attached to each choice.
