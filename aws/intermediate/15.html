Traces are what actually let you find the slow hop

"How would you troubleshoot a slow API request across multiple microservices?"

Why it matters ▾
  • Checks whether you know the real difference between metrics, logs, and traces — and specifically why only traces let you see across service boundaries.
  • Comes up any time a troubleshooting or system-design question turns into "a request is slow, where do you even start looking."
  • A natural opener into what should actually trigger an alarm — separating a real signal from noise.
🔥 Easy interview recall

Metrics → numbers over time

Logs → text for one event

Traces → one request's path across services

Think: metrics say something's slow. logs say what happened at one stop. traces say which stop was slow.

🧠 Visual Mental Model — understand it in 5 seconds
⚠️ Common Trap

"I'll just check each service's own logs and metrics." Without a shared trace ID stitching every hop together, isolated per-service logs only tell you that service was fine or busy — they can't tell you which specific hop in the chain is the one actually making the whole request slow.

🔄 Likely Follow-up

"What should actually trigger an alarm?" A symptom a human needs to act on — an elevated error rate, a p99 latency crossing a real threshold — not every metric that happens to move, which just trains people to ignore alerts.

🎯 Say this in the interview — the exact lines to land

"Metrics, logs, and traces are three different lenses — traces are what actually let you find the slow hop across microservices. I'd pull the trace for the slow request, look at each segment's timing, and see exactly which downstream call is eating the time; metrics and logs then help me confirm and dig into what's happening inside that one service."

  • Metrics → numbers over time — good for "is something wrong right now."
  • Logs → one event's text record — good for "what exactly happened here."
  • Traces → one request's path across every service it touched — good for "which hop is slow."
  • Alarms → symptom-based, like error rate or p99 latency — not every metric that moves.
📖 Explanation ▾

Metrics are numeric measurements tracked over time — CloudWatch stores things like request count, CPU, or latency percentiles as time-series data, aggregated across many requests. Logs are text records of individual events, scoped to whatever wrote them — one service, one line, one moment. Neither one, on its own, can follow a single request as it moves from service to service.

Traces exist to close that gap. AWS X-Ray gives a request one shared trace ID the moment it enters the system, and every instrumented service the request passes through records its own timed segment under that same ID. Correlating a request across microservices depends entirely on that shared trace ID being propagated through every hop — X-Ray's SDK does this automatically once a service is instrumented. Stitched together, those segments become one waterfall view of the whole request, so the slow hop is something you can point at instead of something you have to guess at from separate, disconnected logs.

🌍 Real-world example ▾

A checkout flow feels slow, and the checkout service's own metrics look fine — it's just waiting on something. Pulling the X-Ray trace for one of the slow requests shows the payment-service call is what's actually eating 4 of the request's 5 total seconds, not the checkout service itself, so the investigation moves straight to payment-service instead of wasting time on the wrong team's code.
