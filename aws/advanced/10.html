Rehost, replatform, or refactor — pick per-component, not universally

Rehost vs. replatform vs. refactor — how do you decide, and what would you migrate first?

Why it matters ▾
  • Every real AWS migration project has to answer "how much do we change on the way in" — this is the vocabulary that structures that answer for every single component.
  • It's an easy trap for a candidate to sound impressive by saying "refactor everything" — interviewers use this question specifically to check whether someone can reason about effort vs. benefit instead of reaching for the fanciest-sounding option.
  • Common opener in a migration or cloud-adoption interview, almost always followed by "what would you migrate first, and why."
🔥 Easy interview recall

Rehost → move as-is, fastest

Replatform → optimize during the move

Refactor → redesign for cloud-native

Think: Rehost is speed. Replatform is a tune-up. Refactor is a rebuild.

🧠 Visual Mental Model — understand it in 5 seconds
⚠️ Common Trap

Refactoring everything, regardless of its actual value or risk. Refactor is the most expensive path — a low-value legacy system is often better served by a plain rehost, saving refactoring effort for the components that genuinely benefit from a cloud-native redesign.

🔄 Likely Follow-up

"How do you minimize downtime during migration?" Use DMS's continuous replication to keep the target database in sync with the source right up until cutover — the actual downtime window shrinks to the final switch itself, not a long, blocking data-copy process.

🎯 Say this in the interview — the exact lines to land

"Rehost, replatform, and refactor are an effort-versus-benefit spectrum, not a single right answer — you pick the point on that spectrum per component, not universally across the whole system."

  • Rehost → fastest, lowest risk lift-and-shift; least cloud-native benefit. Good for a deadline-driven exit or a low-value legacy system.
  • Replatform → a targeted optimization during the move, e.g. self-managed database → RDS instead of lifting the database VM as-is.
  • Refactor → most effort, most long-term benefit; redesign for cloud-native patterns like serverless or microservices. Reserve it for systems that actually justify the investment.
  • AWS DMS's continuous replication keeps the target in sync right up to cutover, minimizing downtime to just the final switch.
  • Migrate a monolith with a strangler fig approach — peel off one capability at a time instead of a risky big-bang rewrite.
  • Migrate the lower-risk, well-understood components first, saving the riskiest pieces for once the team has real migration experience.
📖 Explanation ▾

Rehost ("lift and shift") moves a workload onto AWS as-is — e.g. VMs become EC2 instances — with no redesign. It's the fastest option and carries the least migration risk, but it captures the least benefit from cloud-native features, since the architecture itself didn't change. Replatform makes a targeted optimization during the move — most commonly, swapping a self-managed database for a managed equivalent like RDS instead of just lifting the database server as-is. Refactor redesigns the workload for cloud-native patterns, e.g. rearchitecting a monolith into serverless functions or microservices — the most effort, and the most long-term benefit, since the new design can actually take advantage of what the cloud offers.

RehostReplatformRefactor
Effortlowest — no redesignmoderate — targeted changehighest — full redesign
Cloud-native benefitlowest — same architecturemoderate — some optimizationhighest — built for the cloud
Risklowest — least changedmoderatehighest — new design, new failure modes
Typical exampleon-prem VM → EC2, as-isself-managed DB → RDSmonolith → serverless/microservices

These three sit on one effort-vs-benefit spectrum, and the right choice is made per component, not for a whole system at once. AWS DMS (Database Migration Service) supports both a one-time migration and ongoing replication, and its continuous-replication mode is what keeps downtime low: it keeps the target database in sync with the source right up until the actual cutover, so the switch itself is the main interruption. During the migration window, hybrid connectivity — Direct Connect or a VPN — is typically needed to move data and test the target environment before that cutover happens. Before calling any migration complete, the migrated data has to be validated — checksums, row counts, and spot-checking records against the source — not just a copy that merely looks successful.

Migrating a monolith doesn't have to mean a risky big-bang rewrite — the strangler fig approach peels off one capability at a time onto the new architecture while the rest of the monolith keeps running, shrinking the legacy system gradually instead of replacing it all at once. What to migrate first is typically the lower-risk, well-understood components — the riskiest, most complex pieces are saved for later, once the team has actual migration experience behind it.

🌍 Real-world example ▾

A company facing a data-center exit deadline rehosts its legacy internal tools quickly — lift and shift, no redesign, just enough to hit the deadline safely. Separately, over the following year, it refactors its actual customer-facing product into serverless microservices — a much bigger investment, made once the team had real cloud migration experience and confidence behind it, and reserved for the system that actually justified the redesign.
