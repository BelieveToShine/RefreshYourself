HA vs. DR — surviving an instance crash isn't surviving a region crash

"What is high availability, and how is it different from disaster recovery?"

Why it matters ▾
  • Comes up the moment resilience, uptime commitments, or architecture design gets discussed.
  • A very common mix-up — candidates often describe redundant instances as "disaster recovery" when it's actually HA.
  • Usually the opener before deeper questions on RPO/RTO, failover design, or multi-region architecture.
🔥 Easy interview recall

HA → survive a routine failure, same region

DR → survive the whole region going down

Think: HA keeps the lights on. DR is the plan for when the building itself is gone.

🧠 Visual Mental Model — understand it in 5 seconds
⚠️ Common Trap

"We run 3 instances, so we have disaster recovery." That's high availability, not DR — all 3 instances sit in the same region, so if the whole region goes down, all 3 go down with it. DR specifically needs a plan involving another region.

🔄 Likely Follow-up

"What's an Availability Zone?" A physically separate location with independent power, cooling, and networking inside the same region — spreading instances across zones is a common HA technique that doesn't require a second region at all.

🎯 Say this in the interview — the exact lines to land

"High availability survives routine failures within a region, using redundancy across instances and availability zones. Disaster recovery survives losing the whole region, and it's what actually gets measured by RPO and RTO."

  • High availability → redundancy within one region, survives an instance or zone failure.
  • Disaster recovery → a plan to fail over to another region entirely, for a region-wide outage.
  • RPO / RTO → DR's real measurements: how much data loss is acceptable, and how long recovery is allowed to take.
  • Availability Zone → an independent power/cooling/network location inside a region — HA's main tool.
📖 Explanation ▾

High availability is a design goal: keep the service answering through the failures that happen routinely — an instance crashing, a rack losing power, a process needing a restart. It's achieved with redundancy, usually multiple instances spread across several Availability Zones inside the same region, so one failure doesn't take the whole service down with it.

Disaster recovery covers a different scale of problem — not "an instance died" but "the entire region is unreachable." Every zone in that region goes down together, so HA alone can't survive it. DR is the documented (and ideally tested) plan for that case, typically a failover to a separate region, and it's judged by two numbers: RPO (how much data you can afford to lose, measured backward in time from the failure) and RTO (how long you're allowed to take to get the service back up).

🌍 Real-world example ▾

An app runs across 3 Availability Zones for HA day-to-day — that's what keeps it up when a single zone has a bad day. Separately, it has a documented, tested failover runbook to a secondary region as its DR plan, for the day the whole primary region is the problem.
