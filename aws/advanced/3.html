Active/active for reads is easy — active/active for writes needs a real answer to split-brain

Active/active vs. active/passive multi-region — and how do you avoid split-brain?

Why it matters ▾
  • Multi-region questions are a favorite way to separate someone who's read about AWS from someone who has actually reasoned through what happens when two regions can both take traffic at once.
  • Getting active/active vs. active/passive backwards for writes specifically is one of the fastest ways to accidentally propose a design that produces split-brain.
  • A common senior/architect follow-up after "how would you make this system multi-region" — expect it phrased as "what happens if both regions accept a write for the same record at the same time?"
🔥 Easy interview recall

Active/Active → both regions serve live traffic

Active/Passive → one region live, one standby

Think: active/active for reads is easy. Active/active for writes needs one designated writer or a real conflict-resolution plan — or it's split-brain.

🧠 Visual Mental Model — understand it in 5 seconds
⚠️ Common Trap

Designing active/active with independent regional writes and no conflict-resolution strategy. That's exactly the setup that produces split-brain — active/active for reads is far simpler than active/active for writes, and interview answers often conflate the two.

🔄 Likely Follow-up

"What are the cost and operational consequences of going multi-region?" Roughly double (or more) infrastructure cost, plus genuinely harder operations — data consistency, deployment coordination across regions, and testing failover all become permanent ongoing work, not one-time setup.

🎯 Say this in the interview — the exact lines to land

"Active/active for reads is simple; active/active for writes needs a real conflict-resolution strategy or you get split-brain."

  • Route 53 failover routing directs traffic based on health checks between an active region and its standby.
  • Global Accelerator optimizes the network path to your application — routing, not caching.
  • CloudFront caches content at the edge, so many requests never reach either region's origin.
  • Cross-region replication — S3 replication, DynamoDB Global Tables, Aurora Global Database — keeps data available across regions.
  • Split-brain is both regions independently accepting conflicting writes with no way to reconcile which is correct.
  • Going multi-region is roughly double the cost, plus permanent ongoing operational work — not a one-time setup.
📖 Explanation ▾

Active/active means both regions are serving live traffic at the same time. Active/passive means one region is live and the other sits as a standby, ready to take over. For reads, active/active is genuinely simple — CloudFront caches content at the edge so many requests never even reach an origin region, and Global Accelerator improves the network path to whichever region answers, without caching anything itself. Route 53 failover routing watches health checks and redirects traffic to the standby region if the active one goes down — that's the mechanism active/passive relies on.

Writes are where the two strategies stop being symmetric. Split-brain happens when both regions independently accept writes that conflict, with no way to reconcile which one is "correct." Avoiding it takes one of two real answers: keep a single source of truth for writes (one region is the designated writer, and replication — S3 cross-region replication, DynamoDB Global Tables, Aurora Global Database — only flows one way out of it), or genuinely support multiple writers with a real conflict-resolution strategy. Most systems reach for the first option because it's simpler and avoids split-brain entirely.

🌍 Real-world example ▾

A global SaaS company keeps a single "home region" as the write authority. Aurora Global Database replicates that region's data to read replicas in three other regions, so users everywhere get fast local reads. Because writes are never accepted anywhere but the home region, split-brain is avoided entirely — there's only ever one source of truth, not a conflict to resolve.
