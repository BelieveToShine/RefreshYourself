Cost optimization — the levers beyond a cheaper SKU

What are the actual cost-optimization levers beyond just picking a cheaper SKU?

Why it matters ▾
  • A cloud bill review is a real, common architecture conversation — "the bill is too high" is rarely fixed by one setting, it's fixed by matching each workload to the right lever.
  • Interviewers use this question to check whether a candidate thinks in tradeoffs (commitment vs. flexibility, risk vs. discount) instead of just naming a list of Azure features.
  • Misusing one lever — especially committing to Reserved Instances for the wrong kind of workload — is a real, expensive mistake, not a hypothetical one.
🔥 Easy interview recall

Reserved/Savings Plans → commit for steady usage

Spot VMs → cheap, but interruptible

Right-sizing → measure, then downsize

Idle cleanup → delete what nobody uses

Think: match the lever to the workload's pattern — steady, spiky, oversized, or simply forgotten.

⚠️ Common Trap

Reserving capacity always saves money. Not for a workload with unpredictable or spiky usage — the discount only pays off against steady, predictable usage; committing to a reservation for a volatile workload can end up paying for capacity that sits unused.

🔄 Likely Follow-up

"Why are Spot VMs risky for production traffic?" Azure can evict a Spot VM with as little as 30 seconds' notice whenever it needs the capacity back — fine for a batch job that can checkpoint and resume, genuinely dangerous for anything serving live user traffic without a fallback.

🎯 Say this in the interview — the exact lines to land

"Reserved capacity for steady workloads, Spot for interruptible ones, plus right-sizing and idle cleanup — real cost optimization is matching the lever to the workload, not just picking a cheaper SKU."

  • Reserved/Savings Plans → commit 1-3 years for a steady workload — a discount that only pays off on predictable usage.
  • Spot VMs → deep discount for interruptible jobs — Azure can evict them with short notice, so live traffic needs a fallback.
  • Right-sizing → measure real utilization, then downsize — never resize based on a guess.
  • Idle cleanup → tear down dev/test waste — auto-shutdown schedules or deleting unused resource groups, at zero cost to fix.
📖 Explanation ▾

Reserved Instances and Savings Plans trade flexibility for a discount: you commit to 1-3 years of usage up front, and Azure charges a significantly lower rate in exchange. That trade only makes sense when the usage is genuinely steady — a workload that runs at roughly the same size all the time, like a production API's baseline load. Spot VMs go the opposite direction: Azure sells its spare, otherwise-idle capacity at a deep discount, but can reclaim that capacity — and evict your VM — whenever it needs it back for a full-price customer. That makes Spot a great fit for work that can tolerate being interrupted and resumed, and a bad fit for anything that can't.

Right-sizing and idle cleanup are the two levers that cost nothing to apply and are pure upside. Right-sizing means actually measuring CPU/memory/disk utilization over time and matching the SKU to what's really being used, instead of provisioning by guess (or leaving whatever size was picked at launch). Idle cleanup means finding dev/test resources — or entire resource groups — nobody is actively using anymore, and either scheduling auto-shutdown outside business hours or deleting them outright. Neither lever requires committing to anything or accepting any risk; they're just waste that was never noticed.

💻 See the code ▾
CLI 
az vm create \
  --resource-group rg-batch \
  --name batch-worker-01 \
  --image Ubuntu2204 \
  --priority Spot \
  --eviction-policy Deallocate \
  --max-price -1 # -1 = pay up to normal on-demand price, never more

--eviction-policy Deallocate stops the VM instead of deleting it, so the disk survives eviction and the job can resume once capacity frees up.

🌍 Real-world example ▾

A company reserves capacity for its steady-state production API traffic, uses Spot VMs for its nightly batch-processing cluster, and sets auto-shutdown on every dev/test VM outside business hours — three different levers, each matched to a workload that actually fits it.
