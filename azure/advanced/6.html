Landing zones — guardrails before the first resource, not after

What do Azure Policy and management groups actually enforce at an organization level?

Why it matters ▾
  • Real enterprises run dozens or hundreds of subscriptions across many teams — without a shared, inherited set of rules, every team drifts toward its own regions, its own tagging, its own idea of "secure enough."
  • Fixing that drift after the fact means auditing and re-configuring resources that already exist across every team — retrofitting governance is far more expensive than enforcing it from the first resource onward.
  • A common senior/architect governance question — expect it phrased as "how does a real company keep subscription sprawl under control" or "what's actually stopping a team from spinning up resources anywhere they want."
🔥 Easy interview recall

RBAC → who can act

Azure Policy → what's allowed to exist

Think: a landing zone = guardrails already built before the first resource lands, not cleanup applied after.

⚠️ Common Trap

Azure Policy and RBAC do the same job. Not quite — RBAC answers "can this person create a resource at all," while Policy answers "even if they're allowed to, does it meet the rules (region, SKU, required tags)" — they check different things, and a real environment needs both together, not one instead of the other.

🔄 Likely Follow-up

"Why set this up before teams start creating subscriptions, not after?" Retrofitting policy onto hundreds of already-noncompliant resources spread across many teams is far more painful than enforcing the rule from the very first resource onward — landing zones exist specifically to avoid that cleanup debt.

🎯 Say this in the interview — the exact lines to land

"RBAC controls who can act, Policy controls what's allowed to exist — landing zones set both up before teams start creating subscriptions."

  • Retrofitting policy onto hundreds of already-noncompliant resources across many teams is far more painful than enforcing it from resource one.
  • Management groups sit above subscriptions, so policy and RBAC assigned once at that level are inherited by every subscription underneath.
  • Azure Policy enforces or audits rules automatically — deny a region, require a cost-center tag — regardless of who's creating the resource.
  • A landing zone is the pre-built, policy-governed environment a new subscription lands into, guardrails already active.
📖 Explanation ▾

Management groups form a hierarchy above subscriptions — a company might structure it as Company → Business Unit → Subscription. Assigning a policy or an RBAC role at a management group applies it once, and every subscription (and every resource inside it) underneath that group inherits it automatically, without anyone touching each subscription individually.

Azure Policy enforces or audits organizational rules automatically — "deny any resource created outside these two approved regions," "require a cost-center tag on everything." RBAC and Policy answer genuinely different questions: RBAC decides who is allowed to act (can this person create a virtual machine at all), while Policy decides what is allowed to exist or how it must be configured, regardless of who created it. Both are usually needed together, not as substitutes for each other.

A landing zone is the pre-built, policy-governed environment a new subscription or team lands into — the management group hierarchy, the policy assignments, and the RBAC roles are already in place before the first resource is ever created in it, so there's no window where a team can create anything ungoverned.

💻 See the code ▾
Azure CLI 
az policy assignment create --name allowed-regions \
  --policy <policy-definition-id> \       -- the rule being enforced
  --scope <management-group-id>       -- applied once, here

Scoping the assignment to a management group instead of one subscription is what makes it inherited — every current and future subscription under that group picks up the same rule automatically.

🌍 Real-world example ▾

A large enterprise's Azure Policy automatically blocks any resource creation outside two approved regions, so no team can accidentally create infrastructure the compliance team never signed off on. The rule lives once, on a management group, and every subscription underneath it — current and future — is covered without anyone having to remember to apply it again.
