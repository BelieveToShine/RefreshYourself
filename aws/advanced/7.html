NAT Gateway data processing is the cost trap nobody optimizes for

How do you find unexpected AWS spend, and what are the biggest cost traps beyond just picking a cheaper instance type?

Why it matters ▾
  • Cost questions in senior/architect AWS interviews test whether you know where hidden spend actually lives — not just whether you can name a cheaper instance type.
  • A real, recurring production problem: a team spends months rightsizing EC2 instances while NAT Gateway or cross-AZ transfer quietly outgrows the entire compute bill.
  • A common opener before a "your AWS bill just doubled — what do you check first?" scenario question.
🔥 Easy interview recall

Rightsizing → match instance to real usage

Savings Plans / RI → discount, steady load only

Spot → cheap, interruptible capacity

NAT Gateway → billed per GB processed (hidden)

Cross-AZ / Region → also billed per GB (hidden)

Think: obvious levers → rightsizing, commitments, Spot, tiering hidden traps → NAT Gateway + cross-AZ/region transfer, billed per GB nobody watches

🧠 Visual Mental Model — understand it in 5 seconds
⚠️ Common Trap

"Serverless is always cheaper" is not true. Per-request/per-duration billing can cost more than a provisioned server or container once volume is high and steady — the right choice depends on real usage patterns, not on which service is newest.

🔄 Likely Follow-up

"Give me a concrete cost vs. reliability trade-off." Multi-AZ RDS roughly doubles database cost in exchange for automatic failover — worth it for a critical production database, probably not for a low-criticality internal tool; the right call depends on what downtime or data loss actually costs the business.

🎯 Say this in the interview — the exact lines to land

"NAT Gateway data processing and cross-AZ/region transfer are the cost traps that catch people who only optimize compute pricing — the fix is rightsizing plus a real look at data-transfer patterns, backed by Cost Explorer and tagging, not just picking a cheaper instance type."

  • NAT Gateway & cross-AZ/region transfer are billed per GB and are the traps most people never check.
  • Rightsizing means matching instance size to actual utilization, not a guess made at launch time.
  • Savings Plans / Reserved Instances only pay off for genuinely steady, predictable usage.
  • Spot is the right lever for interruptible workloads, not steady production traffic.
  • "Serverless is always cheaper" is false — at high, steady volume a provisioned server can cost less than per-invocation billing.
  • Cost Explorer, budgets, and tagging are how you actually find where the money is going, not guessing.
📖 Explanation ▾

Rightsizing means matching an EC2 instance's size to what the workload actually uses, based on real utilization data, not a guess made when it was launched. Savings Plans and Reserved Instances trade a spend commitment for a discount — they only pay off for genuinely steady, predictable usage; committing capacity for a spiky or short-lived workload can lose money instead of saving it. Spot capacity is priced far below on-demand but can be reclaimed, so it's the right lever for interruptible, fault-tolerant workloads, not steady production traffic. S3 lifecycle rules move data that's gone cold into cheaper storage tiers automatically, without anyone having to remember to do it.

The costs that actually catch people off guard live in data movement, not compute. A NAT Gateway charges per GB of traffic it processes, and that number gets surprisingly large for a chatty, high-volume architecture — a real, commonly-missed line item. Traffic that crosses an Availability Zone or Region boundary is billed per GB too, and it's just as easy to underestimate. CloudFront helps on both fronts at once: fewer requests reach the origin, which lowers both latency and the origin's own compute/transfer cost.

The serverless cost model bills per request and per duration, which is genuinely cheap at low or spiky volume — but "serverless is always cheaper" isn't universally true; at high, steady volume, a provisioned server or container can cost less than paying per invocation. None of this is guesswork: Cost Explorer, budgets/alerts, and cost-allocation tags are how real spend actually gets found, not by assuming which service should be expensive.

🌍 Real-world example ▾

A team running a chatty microservices architecture reviews Cost Explorer and finds NAT Gateway data-processing charges are a bigger line item than anyone expected — services are calling other AWS services across Availability Zones, and every gigabyte is metered. The fix isn't a smaller instance type: they add VPC endpoints for the AWS services being called, routing that traffic privately and avoiding the NAT Gateway entirely for it.
