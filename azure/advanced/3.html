Active-passive or active-active — the business decides, not the engineers

How do RPO and RTO actually drive the choice between an active-passive and an active-active multi-region design?

Why it matters ▾
  • A real disaster-recovery conversation starts as a business conversation — how much data loss and downtime the business can actually survive — and only then becomes an architecture decision.
  • Getting this wrong is expensive in both directions: too little resilience risks a real outage with real data loss; too much burns budget and engineering effort on a guarantee nobody actually asked for.
  • A common senior/architect scenario question — expect it phrased as "how would you design DR for this system," with RPO/RTO as the very first thing you should ask about.
🔥 Easy interview recall

RPO → how much data loss is acceptable

RTO → how long recovery is allowed to take

Active-Passive → cheaper, slower failover

Active-Active → near-zero RTO, real cost & complexity

Think: the business sets the RPO/RTO numbers first — the architecture only exists to hit that target.

🧠 Visual Mental Model — understand it in 5 seconds
⚠️ Common Trap

Active-Active is always the safer choice. It's only the right choice when the business actually needs near-zero RTO — it costs meaningfully more and adds real data-consistency complexity across two live regions, so reaching for it just because it "sounds more resilient" is over-engineering when a 30-minute RTO would have been fine.

🔄 Likely Follow-up

"What actually determines the RPO/RTO numbers?" The business, not the engineering team — a real DR conversation starts with how much data loss and downtime the business can actually survive, then the architecture is chosen to meet that target, not the other way around.

🎯 Say this in the interview — the exact lines to land

"RPO and RTO come from the business, and they decide active-passive vs. active-active — not the other way around. RPO is how much data loss is acceptable, RTO is how long recovery is allowed to take, and the architecture is chosen to hit those numbers."

  • Active-Passive → cheaper, higher RTO — the secondary region sits mostly idle until failover.
  • Active-Active → near-zero RTO, real cost & complexity — both regions serve live traffic, so data consistency between them is a genuine engineering problem.
  • The business sets the target first — the architecture exists to meet it, never picked for its own sake.
  • Active-Active's real cost is consistency across two live regions, not just double the infrastructure bill.
📖 Explanation ▾

RPO (Recovery Point Objective) is how much data loss is acceptable, measured in time — an RPO of 5 minutes means the business can tolerate losing up to 5 minutes of data, which is really a statement about how frequently data has to be replicated to the other region. RTO (Recovery Time Objective) is how long the system is allowed to stay down before it's back up and serving traffic again. Both numbers come from the business, before any architecture gets chosen.

An Active-Passive design keeps a secondary region mostly idle, ready to take over when the primary fails. It's cheaper to run, but failover takes real time — someone or something has to detect the failure and redirect traffic — so RTO is higher, and there's usually some replication lag, so RPO isn't zero either. An Active-Active design has both regions serving live traffic at the same time, so RTO is near-zero — the other region is already handling requests. The tradeoff is cost and complexity: keeping data consistent across two regions that are both being written to at once is a real, ongoing engineering problem, not a one-time setup cost.

🌍 Real-world example ▾

A financial trading system versus an internal reporting tool. A system processing live financial transactions has a near-zero RPO/RTO requirement — even a few minutes of data loss or downtime is unacceptable — so it justifies the cost and complexity of Active-Active. An internal reporting dashboard that a few people check each morning can tolerate a few hours of downtime just fine, so it stays comfortably Active-Passive; paying for Active-Active there would be solving a problem the business never actually had.
