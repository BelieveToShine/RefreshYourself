Pilot light, warm standby, active/active — the business picks, not the engineers

"Pilot light vs. warm standby vs. multi-site active/active — how would you choose a DR strategy?"

Why it matters ▾
  • Checks whether you know a DR strategy is chosen by the business's RPO/RTO requirement, not by engineering preference for the fanciest option.
  • Comes up any time the conversation turns to "how would you keep this running if a whole region went down."
  • A natural follow-up after any high-availability, multi-region, or replication question.
🔥 Easy interview recall

Backup & Restore → cheapest, slowest

Pilot Light → core stays on

Warm Standby → full stack, scaled down

Active/Active → both regions live

Think: each step right means faster recovery, but also higher cost and more to run.

⚠️ Common Trap

Active/active is not automatically the "best" DR strategy. It's meaningfully more expensive and complex than pilot light or warm standby, and it's overkill if the business can tolerate a slower recovery — the right pick is whichever strategy actually meets the required RPO/RTO, not the most impressive-sounding one.

🔄 Likely Follow-up

"How would you actually test DR?" Regularly and realistically — actually failing over to the DR region, not just reviewing the plan on paper, is the only way to know the runbook and the infrastructure genuinely work; untested DR plans routinely fail when actually needed.

🎯 Say this in the interview — the exact lines to land

"RPO/RTO requirements from the business decide the DR strategy — pilot light, warm standby, or active/active, in increasing cost order. And I'd actually test failover, not just review the plan on paper."

  • RPO → how much data loss is acceptable RTO → how long recovery is allowed to take
  • Backup & Restore → cheapest, slowest — restore from backups into a new environment when disaster hits.
  • Pilot Light → core infra always on, everything else scales up only on failover.
  • Warm Standby → full stack, scaled down, scaled up to full capacity on failover.
  • Active/Active → both regions live — lowest RTO/RPO, highest cost and complexity.
  • Actually fail over to test DR — a plan only reviewed on paper, never run for real, routinely fails.
📖 Explanation ▾

RPO (Recovery Point Objective) is how much data loss the business can accept, measured backward in time from the disaster. RTO (Recovery Time Objective) is how long the business can accept being down, measured forward from the disaster until service is back. Every DR strategy is really just a different tradeoff between how small it can make those two numbers and how much it costs to run all the time.

The four common strategies sit on one spectrum. Backup and restore keeps only backups — recovering means standing up a brand-new environment from scratch, which is cheapest but slowest. Pilot light keeps a small core (often just a database) running in the DR region all the time, with everything else scaled up only when failover actually happens. Warm standby goes further: a scaled-down but fully functional copy of the whole stack is already running in the DR region, so failover is mostly a scale-up, not a build-out. Multi-site active/active keeps both regions fully live at the same time, serving real traffic — the fastest and most expensive option. DynamoDB Global Tables and S3 cross-region replication are the practical mechanisms that keep data ready in the DR region for any of these strategies, so the app doesn't wake up to stale or missing data.

🌍 Real-world example ▾

A company running a warm standby DR environment finally does a real failover test instead of just reviewing the runbook on paper — and discovers a manual DNS step nobody had automated, one that would have added real downtime during an actual disaster. The environment itself was fine; the gap was in a step nobody had ever actually rehearsed.
